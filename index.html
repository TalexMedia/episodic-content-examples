Episodic Branded Content

Thirteen examples of video that a brand paid for and an audience chose to watch, and what each one shows about building a format that lasts.

Most brand video gets made once, published, and forgotten. The examples here work differently, because each one is a format rather than a film: a repeatable idea with a new subject every time, so the audience knows what it is getting and comes back for the next one.

The page is grouped by what each example demonstrates: how a format repeats, where the brand sits inside the story, and what the form can do at different budgets. The range runs from a single creator with a small crew up to a scripted short film and an in-house studio slate.

On the numbers. Every view count, episode count and channel figure was pulled from the YouTube API on 29 July 2026. Those figures move, so read them as a snapshot rather than a fixed result. Where a number comes from a brand's own award entry instead of a public source, the card says so plainly.

01

Formats That Repeat

One idea, a new subject each time. These four were built to run for years rather than for a campaign.

Repeatable Format · Eight Seasons
The Weekender
  • A host helps one homeowner finish a room or a yard over a single weekend. Lowe's started it in 2016 and it has run for at least eight seasons across Apple TV, Fire TV, Roku and YouTube.
  • The episode below comes from season eight, runs 23 minutes and has 95,821 views.
  • What it shows: one clear constraint, a single weekend, has carried a retailer's series for the better part of a decade without the format needing to change.
The Weekender · Lowe's

Repeatable Format · 245 Videos
IKEA Home Tour
  • Five IKEA employees travel the country and make over rooms in real families' homes, working to whatever problem that household actually has. Using staff rather than celebrities keeps the results looking reachable.
  • The makeovers playlist now holds 245 videos with 59.6 million views between them, counting the full episodes alongside trailers and shorter tips.
  • The three most watched episodes all run between three and five minutes, well ahead of the longer ten to fifteen minute ones.
  • What it shows: a format can scale to hundreds of videos, and the short cuts can outperform the full episodes by a wide margin.

Repeatable Format · Software Brand
Make My Space Work
  • The designer Alexandra Gater rebuilds one entrepreneur's workspace in each episode, working to a budget and around how that person actually runs their business.
  • Shopify Studios makes it, and the series has its own channel separate from both Shopify's and the designer's, carrying 26 videos and five million views.
  • That channel has 61,200 subscribers against those five million views, so most of the audience arrives through search and recommendations rather than by following it.
  • What it shows: a software company with nothing physical to film can still build a watchable series, by putting its customers on camera instead of its product.

02

Where The Brand Sits

Four ways of putting a product inside a story, from the center of the plot through to almost invisible.

Integration · Product As The Plot
Between 2 Rides
  • Two versions of the same car go side by side, one left stock and one modified with parts sourced through eBay, then get tested against each other.
  • Season two ran five episodes with seven builder-creators, and eBay cut more than 200 social assets from the same footage for YouTube, Instagram and TikTok.
  • What it shows: when the product causes the difference the episode is built around, the integration needs no voiceover to explain it. It also shows how far one shoot can stretch across channels.

Integration · A Decade-Long Sponsorship
Roadkill
  • Two former car-magazine editors buy junk cars, get them running again on camera, and then drive them somewhere far away. Dodge came on as sponsor in 2015.
  • The launch episode below is a 39-minute thrash between three new Dodge cars, and its description opens with the line "Roadkill is now powered by Dodge!" so the arrangement is declared rather than hidden.
  • After that the sponsor stays light. Across the 142 videos in the run only 8 titles name Dodge or one of its models, and the rest are junk cars of every make.
  • What it shows: a sponsor can announce itself once and then step back, and the goodwill lasts. The run has 187.7 million views across it and the live event it spawned was still running ten years later.

Integration · Product Almost Absent
YETI Presents
  • Short documentary films about the kind of people who use the gear, including anglers, hunters, cooks and surfers. They are built around personal stories and barely mention the product.
  • The library now holds 90 films with 37.6 million views between them. They are sorted by what the viewer does rather than by release order, with separate collections for fishing, hunting, surfing, mountains, rodeo and cooking.
  • The film below runs 28 minutes and is the most watched of all 90.
  • What it shows: a library organized around the audience's interests rather than a release schedule keeps earning views for years, and the longest film can be the best performing one.

Integration · Brand As A Point Of View
Living Off The Wall
  • Portraits of skaters, artists, musicians and other people living outside the mainstream, funded by Vans and released on its own channel.
  • The shoes are barely mentioned and the subjects wore their own rather than being dressed by the brand.
  • What it shows: a brand can pay for a documentary strand that carries its point of view without putting its product on screen at all. What is being advertised is the outlook, not the item.

03

Format And Scale

What else the form can do, from a casting idea that needs almost no script through to a scripted film and a studio slate.

Format · Casting Carries The Story
Make Up Over Makeup
  • Two people with unresolved tension between them do each other's makeup while talking through what went wrong, across three episodes.
  • Every pair had already fallen out in public before filming, so the backstory was out there before episode one. Doing someone's makeup also means sitting inches apart, which makes the conversation hard to avoid.
  • The first two pairs were creators who had documented their relationship and then split, and two castmates from Vanderpump Rules (the Bravo series set around Lisa Vanderpump's Los Angeles restaurants).
  • The third was a couple from Love Is Blind (the Netflix series where couples get engaged before meeting in person) whose wedding did not happen.
  • What it shows: casting can do the work a script would otherwise have to. The third episode drew four times the first and sixteen times the second, and it was also the most recent and most public falling out.

Format · An Ad With No Script
Oddly IKEA
  • Twenty-five minutes of ASMR, all whispering and soft tapping, shot around bedding, storage boxes and a desk lamp, and made as a back-to-college ad for dorm rooms.
  • There is no story and no presenter delivering a script, just the products being handled, and it holds 3.5 million views with 81,138 likes.
  • What it shows: a piece of advertising can borrow a native internet format outright and hold attention far longer than an ad normally would. This one runs longer than most of the documentaries on this page.

Scale · Scripted Short Film
Two Bellmen
  • Scripted action-comedy shorts about two hotel bellmen, set in and around Marriott properties and made by the brand's own content studio.
  • Three films in the run, and the first runs 18 minutes and now has 5,276,888 views.
  • What it shows: at the upper end of the scale a brand can commission genuine narrative filmmaking and use its own premises as the set, so the location budget is already paid for.

Scale · Brand As Studio
Second Act
  • Mailchimp launched an in-house entertainment slate of original films, series and podcasts for small-business owners, made with partners including Vice and the production company behind Queer Eye (the Netflix makeover series).
  • The episode below comes from Second Act, the documentary strand about people who changed direction in their working lives. It sits on VICE TV's channel rather than on Mailchimp's.
  • What it shows: the far end of the scale, where a brand funds a whole slate and lets a distribution partner's channel carry it. The trade is reach in exchange for the content not living on your own channel.

And One That Went The Other Way

The same ingredients as the examples above, without a format to hold them together.

Counter-Example · A Roster, Not A Series
DIY Influencer Program
  • Eleven designers and DIY creators make over spaces using Sherwin-Williams paint, built around the annual Color of the Year, with a charitable strand in which they repaint shelters and community centers.
  • There is no series and no format holding it together. The creators post to their own blogs and social accounts, and what sits on the brand's own channel is short before-and-after clips like the one-minute film below.
  • What it shows: good creators and a real budget are not enough on their own. Without a repeatable format the output is a scatter of clips, and none of them reach the numbers the formatted series on this page do.

05

What Runs Through All Of Them

The format is the asset, not the film. Every example that lasted has one sentence a viewer can repeat: a room in a weekend, two versions of the same car, a garden built one stage at a time. The ones without that sentence did not build an audience.

The brand can sit anywhere on the scale. It can be the plot, as with the eBay builds, or almost absent, as with the YETI films. What stays constant is that the product is never the reason to watch. It earns its place by being useful to what is happening on screen.

Repetition is what builds the habit. A single film gets one launch, while a format gets a new launch every time it returns, and the back catalog keeps earning views in the meantime. The IKEA and Roadkill libraries are still collecting views years after the cameras stopped.

Scale is a choice, not a barrier. The examples here run from one creator with a small crew up to a scripted short film. Several of the strongest performers on this page were also among the cheapest to make.
